\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The paradox is an inference problem}

The universe is old, planets are everywhere, and a great many planetary systems
were finished billions of years before ours got started
\citep{lineweaver2001,borucki2016}. Nobody has ever confirmed a signal, a probe,
an artefact, or an unambiguous technosignature. The tension between those two
statements is what we call the Fermi paradox, and it usually gets compressed
into three words: where is everybody? \citep{hart1975,brin1983silence,webb2015}

Put that way, the question is misleading, because it quietly treats
non-observation as evidence about existence. It is not. Non-observation
constrains a \emph{product}: how many civilizations arise, how many last long
enough to be technologically active, how strong a signature they put out, and
whether anyone has yet pointed an instrument at the part of parameter space
where that signature would show up \citep{gray2015,tarter2001,sheikh2020}. The
sharper question is which assumptions would make an extraterrestrial
technological civilization visible \emph{to us, now}.

This is not a rhetorical distinction. It decides what a model of the paradox
ought to vary. Almost every quantitative treatment, from Drake onward, varies
the number of civilizations while holding detectability fixed --- usually
bundled into a single lifetime parameter $L$ \citep{prantzos2013,prantzos2020}.
We do the other thing. We hold abundance still and give detectability its own
dynamics.

\subsection{Existence, detectability, observation}

Three quantities get conflated constantly, so we will separate them at the
start:
\begin{equation}
  \Ntrue \;\geq\; \Ndet \;\geq\; \Nobs ,
  \label{eq:threeN}
\end{equation}
the number of civilizations that exist, the number putting out signatures
detectable in principle, and the number our searches have actually found. A
civilization can exist without being detectable. It can be detectable in
principle and never be observed, simply because nobody looked in the right
place. \Established

This framework modifies the middle term. It asks how a civilization's external
observability changes as its capability, regulatory capacity, energy use,
expansion behaviour and information efficiency change --- and in particular what
happens when it crosses into recursive, self-improving technological capability.

\subsection{Why recursive capability is the interesting transition}

Something changes in kind when a civilization's tools start improving the
process of making better tools. Automated discovery, engineering and design
close a loop between capability and the \emph{rate of growth} of capability
\citep{good1965,bostrom2014,russell2019}. Quantum computation enters this
picture in exactly one way, and it is worth being precise about it: not as a
separate phenomenon, but as one possible contributor to how fast that loop can
turn, by accelerating the simulation, search and optimisation problems that gate
technological progress \citep{shor1994,nielsen2010,preskill2018}. \Speculative

A loop like that plausibly changes more than a growth rate. It may change
information efficiency, and so signal leakage. It may change whether expansion
looks worth doing. It may stretch or compress the window during which a
civilization is loud. None of that is asserted here as fact. The model asks what
\emph{follows} if such feedback occurs, and keeps the dependence on that
assumption out in the open where it can be attacked.

\subsection{What breaks if observability is modelled carelessly}

Giving observability its own differential equation is easy to do badly, and the
way it fails is instructive enough that we would rather report it than hide it.

Suppose you write the terms representing compression, efficiency and deliberate
quietness as an absolute subtraction --- a flux removed from $\Obs$ at a rate
that does not depend on $\Obs$. Then nothing whatsoever prevents observability
from sailing through zero and running off to large negative values. Worse, a
trajectory that has done this still gets classified as ``quiet'', because the
classifier only ever asks whether $\Obs$ is below a threshold, and $-1431$ is
certainly below a threshold.

That is not a coding slip. It is a modelling error, and it has a clean physical
diagnosis: compression and stealth reduce the signal a civilization
\emph{emits}. They are fractional reductions. You cannot suppress emissions that
are not being made. Write them as fractional rates acting on the emitted signal,
put in an explicit floor for the waste heat nobody can get rid of, and
observability becomes bounded below by construction rather than by a clamp
bolted on afterwards. We prove this as Proposition~\ref{prop:obs}, with the
matching statement for regulatory capacity as Proposition~\ref{prop:reg}.

The correction turns out to carry the main modelling result of the paper. Once
suppression is fractional, the quasi-steady observability is a \emph{ratio} of
production to suppression --- and peak-then-decline behaviour then follows
automatically whenever suppression grows faster in capability than production
does. It no longer has to be imposed by picking a peaked functional form. That
matters, because a low-observability account of the Great Silence that assumes a
peaked $\Obs(\Icap)$ has assumed its conclusion. One that derives the peak from
two independently motivated mechanisms has not.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item An observability-aware decomposition
    $\Nobs = \Ntrue\,\Psurv\,h(\Obs)\,\Psearch$ that separates survival, signal
    production and search coverage, and says exactly where a dynamical
    detectability model acts on the Drake equation (\S\ref{sec:decomp}).
  \item A three-variable dynamical model of capability, regulation and
    observability, built from mechanism balances, with two structural
    guarantees --- $\Obs \geq \Ofloor$ and $\Reg \in [0,1]$ --- that hold by
    construction and not by clamping (\S\ref{sec:model}).
  \item Peak-then-decline observability as a \emph{derived} consequence of
    suppression outgrowing production, rather than an assumed functional form
    (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A registry architecture in which every functional form is
    interchangeable, so that ``do the conclusions survive changing the
    functions?'' becomes a menu selection instead of a rewrite
    (\S\ref{sec:model}).
  \item A verification protocol that is unusual in this literature: closed-form
    solutions, an independent high-order integrator, quadrature, and
    property-based testing over randomised parameter sets
    (\S\ref{sec:verification}).
  \item An explicitly negative result about the Lambert-$W$ threshold that
    started this work: the balance justifying $W$ is not the balance governing
    the system we integrate (\S\ref{sec:lambert}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What this paper does not claim}

It does not claim that extraterrestrial civilizations exist, that advanced
civilizations go quiet, that recursive artificial intelligence causes collapse
or transcendence, or that the Fermi paradox has been solved. Every substantive
statement in what follows carries a strength label --- \Established, \Plausible,
\Hypothetical{} or \Speculative{} --- and they are all collected in
Table~\ref{tab:claims} so that nobody has to hunt for the weak ones.
