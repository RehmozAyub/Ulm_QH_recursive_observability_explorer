All results below use the parameter set in Appendix~\ref{app:params} and are

\subsection{Stage trajectories}

Figure~\ref{fig:stages} shows capability, regulation and observability for the
eight canonical stage configurations. Table~\ref{tab:stagecheck} compares the
outcome of each against the behaviour that stage is \emph{defined} to exhibit ---
a consistency check we regard as a minimum standard, and one that a model can
easily fail without anyone noticing.

Two configurations are worth reading closely. Stage~1, the early technological
phase, reduces to a logistic capability equation with no regulatory damping, and
reaches its analytic limits exactly: $\Icap \to aA/s_I = 3.0$ and
$\Obs \to 1-e^{-\lambda \Icap} = 0.5934$ (Appendix~\ref{app:closed}). Stage~4a
drives regulation to $\Reg_\infty = 0.0438$, below the collapse threshold
$\Rmin=0.05$, \emph{without $\Reg$ becoming negative} --- collapse proceeds by
the intended mechanism of regulatory exhaustion rather than by a sign inversion
in the damping term.

\subsection{Peak-then-decline is derived, not imposed}
\label{sec:derived}

This is the central result. Figure~\ref{fig:suppression} contrasts two
formulations of the observability equation on the same stage-4c configuration.

Panel (a) uses an absolute suppression term, $-mC-nS$, whose rate does not depend
on $\Obs$. Observability passes through zero at $\tau\approx0.3$ and falls
without bound, reaching $-1431$ by the end of the integration. Under the
criteria of Table~\ref{tab:regimes} this trajectory is still labelled
``optimized low-observable'', because the classifier reads $\Obs < \Odet$. The
label is arithmetically correct and physically vacuous: the civilization is not
quiet, it is off the bottom of the model.

Panel (b) uses the fractional form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dO}. Observability rises from
$0.100$ to a peak of $0.231$ at $\tau=0.65$, then decays to $0.037$ --- below the
detection threshold, above the thermodynamic floor, and strictly positive
throughout.

The important point is \emph{why} it declines. Nothing in this configuration
selects a peaked functional form; $\Obs$ is integrated, not evaluated. The decline
follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:decline}: compression and stealth scale as
$\Icap^{2}\Reg$ while production scales as $\Icap$, so the quasi-steady
observability $\Obs^{*} \sim 1/(\Icap\Reg)$ falls as capability grows. A
civilization becomes harder to see because efficiency outpaces output, which is a
mechanism rather than a stipulation. \Plausible

This distinction matters for the standing objection to low-observability accounts
of the Great Silence --- that they assume what they set out to explain. A model
that selects a peaked $\Obs(\Icap)$ does assume it. One that obtains the peak
from the ratio of two independently motivated terms does not, and can be
falsified by showing that the required scaling separation is implausible.

\subsection{Robustness across observability models}

Figure~\ref{fig:obsmodels} shows the four static observability models of
Table~\ref{tab:registry}. These span monotone-increasing, monotone-decreasing,
peaked and threshold behaviour, and the framework admits all of them.

The relevant robustness question is not which curve is ``right'' but whether a
conclusion survives changing it. The low-observability regime is reachable under
the peaked and decreasing models and under the dynamical form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:dO}; it is \emph{not} reachable under the increasing model, which
instead produces the expansionist-visible regime seen in stage~4b. We regard
this as the correct behaviour: a framework in which every functional choice
yielded the same conclusion would not be testing anything. \Established

\subsection{Phase structure}

Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows the trajectories in the $(\Icap,\Reg)$ plane. Because
regulation is confined to $[0,1]$, the phase space is a bounded strip and the
regime boundaries are directly readable: the vertical line at $\Iadv$ separates
pre-advanced from advanced capability, and the horizontal line at $\Rmin$ marks
regulatory exhaustion.

The branch structure of Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} is visible here. Configurations
sharing an initial condition separate according to whether regulation is
sustained: those retaining $\Reg\gtrsim0.5$ move right along the strip into the
advanced regimes, while stage~4a falls to the lower boundary and triggers the
collapse proxy. \Hypothetical

\subsection{Decomposing the silence}

Finally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} lets us ask which factor a null result actually
constrains. Taking stage~4c as an illustration, with $\Obs=0.037$,
$\kappa=1$ and $\Psearch=0.5$, the single-civilization detection probability is
\begin{equation}
  \Pdet = \big(1-e^{-\kappa \Obs}\big)\Psearch = 0.018 ,
\end{equation}
against $\Pdet = 0.50$ for the expansionist stage~4b at $\Obs=27$. The same
underlying population is therefore some 27 times less likely to be detected in
the quiet regime than in the loud one, with no change in $\Ntrue$ and no
extinction event. \Hypothetical

We stress what this does \emph{not} show. It does not show that civilizations are
quiet, nor that quietness explains the Great Silence. It shows that under this
framework a null result is consistent with a wide range of $\Ntrue$, and that
attributing non-detection to rarity requires an argument about $\Obs$ and
$\Psearch$ that is usually left implicit.
