\section{The simulator, and how we checked it}
\label{sec:verification}

Models like this one are usually verified about as far as ``the code ran and the
plots looked reasonable''. That is not enough here. Every conclusion in
\S\ref{sec:results} is a statement about the \emph{shape} of a computed
trajectory, so if the trajectory is wrong the conclusion is wrong, and it will
be wrong in a way that still looks perfectly plausible on a screen. We therefore
treat verification as part of the contribution rather than as housekeeping.

\subsection{The simulator}

The framework is packaged as an interactive simulator. Every driver, weight and
threshold in Appendix~\ref{app:params} is exposed as a control; every entry in
the registry of Table~\ref{tab:registry} is a menu selection; and the eight
stage presets of \S\ref{sec:results} load with one click. Trajectories, the
phase portrait, the regime label and the detection probability $\Pdet$ all
update as the parameters move.

We mention this because it is the part of the work that is hardest to convey on
paper. A reader who suspects that some result here is an artefact of a
particular parameter choice can go and drag the slider. The registry means that
``does this conclusion survive a different functional form?'' takes a few
seconds to answer instead of an afternoon of editing, and we would rather people
attacked the model that way than took our word for it. The simulator runs in a
browser with nothing to install; the address is given at the end of the paper.

\subsection{Integration}

The system \eqref{eq:dI}--\eqref{eq:dO} is integrated with an explicit adaptive
Dormand--Prince Runge--Kutta method of order 5(4) with dense output
\citep{dormand1980,virtanen2020scipy,harris2020numpy}, at relative tolerance
$10^{-6}$ and absolute tolerance $10^{-9}$. All exponentials are
argument-clipped to $[-50,50]$ to stop them overflowing.

Two of the recursion kernels in Table~\ref{tab:registry} --- superlinear and
exponential --- blow up in finite time: $\Icap$ genuinely diverges at a finite
$\tau$. An adaptive stepper walking into a singularity takes smaller and smaller
steps and never arrives, so integration stops on a terminal event when $\Icap$
exceeds a bound $I_{\mathrm{abort}}$ set two orders of magnitude above the
runaway threshold, and the remaining output samples hold the terminal state.

That last detail is worth a sentence, because we got it wrong first. If you pad
instead with the last \emph{output} sample, the recorded maximum can understate
the divergence by orders of magnitude --- the singularity almost always falls
between two samples, and the trajectory looks merely large rather than infinite.

\subsection{Five independent oracles}

Each check below tests the implementation against something that is not the
implementation. Results are collected in Table~\ref{tab:verification}.

\paragraph{A. Closed-form regulation.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dR} is linear in $\Reg$ and --- this is the useful part ---
completely independent of $\Icap$ and $\Obs$. So it integrates exactly, giving
$\Reg(\tau)=\Reg_\infty+(\Reg_0-\Reg_\infty)e^{-(B+W)\tau}$ with $\Reg_\infty$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:Rinf}. Every configuration reproduces it to
$\leq 2.3\times10^{-6}$, which is what the requested tolerance should give.

\paragraph{B. An independent high-order integrator.}
Every configuration was integrated again with an eighth-order Dormand--Prince
method at tolerances $10^{-12}/10^{-14}$ and compared against the production
solver at its default settings. All twenty agree. For the two blow-up kernels
the comparison stops at the abort event, since the reference integrator has no
such guard and simply keeps going.

\paragraph{C. Quadrature of the observability equation.}
$\mathrm{d}\Obs/\mathrm{d}\tau$ was rebuilt from the computed $\Icap$ and $\Reg$
and integrated by quadrature, which never touches the ODE solver's own treatment
of $\Obs$. Agreement is $\leq 1.5\times10^{-4}$ on every non-singular
configuration.

\paragraph{D. The Lambert identity.}
$\Icrit$ has to satisfy its own definition, $\Icrit e^{\Icrit} = C_R/(b\Arec)$.
Across 576 parameter combinations and four values of $\Reg$ --- 2304 evaluations
--- the largest relative residual is $4.4\times10^{-16}$, which is machine
precision. The validity guard behaves as specified too, returning no boundary at
all for any non-Lambert kernel.

\paragraph{E. Property-based testing of the invariants.}
This is the one that matters most. Propositions~\ref{prop:reg}
and~\ref{prop:obs} claim \emph{structural} bounds, so they have to hold for
arbitrary admissible parameters --- not for the eight presets we happened to
tune. Three hundred randomised parameter sets were drawn across the full
declared ranges, with randomised recursion kernels and randomised suppression
forms. There were \textbf{no violations}: the global minimum observability came
out at $2.16\times10^{-3}$, comfortably above the floor, and regulation stayed
inside $[4.0\times10^{-3},\,0.999]$.

A bound that held only for the configurations we chose would not be a bound. It
would be a coincidence we had grown fond of. This test is the difference.

\subsection{What this establishes, and what it does not}

These checks establish that the equations we have written down are the equations
being solved, and that they are being solved accurately. They establish exactly
nothing about whether those equations describe a real civilization. Not one term
here has been calibrated against data, for the excellent reason that no relevant
data exist.

The distinction between a model that is \emph{verified} and one that is
\emph{validated} is worth keeping sharp, and it gets blurred constantly in this
subject. We claim the first and make no claim at all to the second.
